Living in a small apartment doesn't automatically rule out an infrared sauna — but it does change how you shop. A homeowner measures the garage and picks the roomiest 2-person cabin they can afford. An apartment dweller works backwards from constraints: the corner that's actually free, the lease terms, the building's doorways, the outlet situation, and the neighbor below. Get those right and a sauna in a one-bedroom apartment is completely realistic. Get them wrong and you've got an 800-pound delivery problem in a third-floor walk-up.

This guide walks through the apartment-specific checklist in the order that matters — permission first, then space, then the pick — and finishes with an honest verdict on when a cabin makes sense and when a sauna blanket is the smarter move. If you're still choosing a model, pair this with our [choosing guide](/posts/how-to-choose-infrared-sauna/) and the [electrical requirements](/posts/infrared-sauna-electrical-requirements/) breakdown.

## Step 1: Get landlord permission in writing

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters most. A cabin sauna isn't a refrigerator — it's a large appliance your landlord didn't know about when they wrote the lease. Even though a plug-and-play infrared cabin changes nothing about the apartment permanently (no wiring changes, no plumbing, no steam), you want permission on the record before the delivery truck arrives.

How to make the ask land: frame it as a plug-in appliance, not a renovation. The facts are on your side — standard 120V outlet, no electrician, no steam or splash moisture (infrared heat works nothing like a wet steam room), no wall mounting, no floor anchoring. A few hundred pounds spread across a 28" x 35.5" base is trivially within what residential floors are designed to carry. Write the request in an email so the approval is documented.

If your landlord says no, or your lease has a blanket prohibition on "appliances" beyond standard household items, don't fight it — skip straight to the sauna blanket. It draws less power than a hair dryer, makes no noise, produces no moisture, and needs no approval in most leases. The [HigherDOSE blanket](amazon:higherdose-sauna-blanket) is purpose-built for exactly this situation.

## Step 2: Verify the space and the delivery route

Measuring the room is only half the job. Measure the path the box takes too.

**The room.** The Elite needs 28"W x 35.5"D plus a few inches on each side — clearance for the door swing, panel ventilation, and your own ability to get the panels into position during assembly. Realistic spots: a bedroom corner, a home-office corner, the area near a walk-in closet, or a large closet with the door removed (closet installs are popular — the cabin is enclosed anyway). Run your numbers through our [space fit checker](/tools/space-fit-checker/) before you order.

**The ceiling.** The Elite is 65" tall assembled — fine under any standard ceiling — but confirm you have enough room to tilt panels upright during assembly. Some cabins need a few extra inches above their final height while panels are being seated.

**The delivery route.** Cabin saunas ship as flat-packed panels in large boxes. Measure your building's entrance door, any elevator (door and cab), every hallway corner, and the apartment door. Panels arrive flat so they often fit where a fully assembled unit wouldn't, but a third-floor walk-up with a tight stairwell is where apartment sauna plans go to die. If the boxes can't make the route, the cabin can't live in your apartment — and that's your signal to take the blanket route instead. The blanket arrives in a box roughly the size of a large suitcase and rolls under an arm.

**Floor load.** Cabins weigh a few hundred pounds (plus you). That's comfortably within residential floor design capacity — this is a non-issue in almost every apartment building — but if you're in an older building or have any doubt, it's worth one sentence in the landlord permission email.

## Step 3: Confirm the electrical situation

This is the easiest item on the list: nearly every cabin in our guide runs on a standard 120V/15A outlet, the same kind your phone charger uses. The [Dynamic Elite](amazon:dynamic-elite-1-person) and [Barcelona](amazon:dynamic-barcelona-1-2-person) are both plug-and-play — no electrician, no panel work, no landlord-visible modifications.

The one rule: buy on a dedicated outlet if you can. Infrared cabins draw real continuous power during heat-up (over 1,000W for cabins), so sharing the circuit with a space heater or hair dryer can trip the breaker. A bedroom or home-office outlet that isn't shared with high-draw appliances is ideal. Our [electrical requirements guide](/posts/infrared-sauna-electrical-requirements/) walks through circuit math in detail — and if you ever consider a 20A model like the HeatWave Santa Fe, don't; it needs an electrician and is a non-starter in most rentals.

Running costs stay apartment-friendly too: a 1-person cabin costs roughly $0.16–$0.23 per session at typical electricity rates, and the blanket is about $0.05. ## Step 4: Ventilation and the neighbor test

Two apartment anxieties, both overblown for infrared:

**Ventilation.** Infrared saunas don't generate steam, so there's no moisture-load problem for your walls or the unit below. What you do want is ordinary airflow — a cracked door nearby or a ceiling fan — because an enclosed heated cabin in a small room warms the room around it over a 30–40 minute session. Crack the cabin door for a few minutes after your session and the room recovers fast.

**Noise.** Infrared cabins are near-silent. There's no compressor, no water pump, no heavy equipment — just heaters radiating and a small circulation fan that's quieter than a laptop fan. Your neighbors will not hear it. The only smell is fresh hemlock or cedar for the first several sessions, which reads as "woodshop," not "complaint." On the noise front, a cabin sauna is a better apartment citizen than a treadmill, a dishwasher, or honestly most televisions.
