Choosing between an infrared and a traditional sauna is really a choice between two different experiences — not a contest with one winner. They heat differently, install differently, cost differently, and feel completely different to sit in. If you've only ever seen the wooden cabin style and wondered what "infrared" actually changes, this guide lays it all out honestly so you can buy the right one the first time.

## How they heat: air vs. body

A traditional sauna — the Finnish-style kind — works by heating the air. An electric (or sometimes wood-fired) heater warms a stack of rocks, and the rocks radiate heat into the room until the air itself is hot: typically 150–195°F. You sweat because the air around you is scorching. Pouring water over the rocks creates a burst of steam called löyly, which spikes the humidity and makes the heat feel suddenly much more intense. The whole ritual — the heat, the steam, the cooldown — is built around that cycle.

An infrared sauna works the opposite way. Instead of heating the air, carbon or ceramic panels emit infrared radiation that warms your body directly, similar to how sunlight warms you on a cool day. The cabin air stays much cooler — usually around 120–140°F — while you still sweat profusely because the radiant heat penetrates the skin. There's no steam, no rocks, and no water ritual.

What does this mean in practice? Infrared sessions feel gentler and more tolerable for longer. Traditional sessions feel more intense and "classic," with the heat hitting your face and lungs the moment you open the door. If you've ever found a gym sauna overwhelming at 180°F+, you may genuinely prefer the infrared style — and if you love that blast-furnace feeling, infrared will feel tame by comparison.

## Installation and electrical requirements

This is where the two diverge most dramatically, and it's the single biggest reason many people choose infrared.

Most Amazon-tier infrared cabins are genuinely plug-and-play: they run on a standard 120V/15A household outlet. You wheel the flat-packed cabin into a spare room or garage, assemble it (usually a few hours with a partner), plug it in, and you're done. No electrician, no permits, no new wiring. That's a huge part of their appeal — our [infrared sauna electrical requirements](/posts/infrared-sauna-electrical-requirements/) guide walks through the specifics, but the short version is that most buyers never call anyone.

Traditional saunas are a different project. Their heaters typically demand 220V on a dedicated circuit, which means hiring an electrician to run new wiring from your breaker panel. Depending on your home's panel capacity and how far the sauna sits from it, that can be a meaningful addition to the budget — and a real barrier for renters. Traditional saunas also benefit from more deliberate ventilation planning, since you're moving hot, humid air around rather than just warming a dry box.

If you rent, live in an apartment, or simply don't want a home-improvement project attached to your purchase, infrared is the only practical choice of the two.

## Cost: purchase price and running costs

Infrared wins on entry cost by a clear margin. Wooden infrared cabins from the brands sold on Amazon typically land in the ~$1,199–$2,500 range for 1–2 person models — see our roundup of the [best infrared saunas of 2026](/posts/best-infrared-saunas-2026/) for current options, or our [best infrared saunas under $1,000](/posts/best-infrared-sauna-under-1000/) if budget is the priority. Installation cost is effectively zero: assembly is DIY and there's no electrician to pay.

Traditional saunas typically start around $3,000 installed, and the total climbs quickly once you add the electrician's work, ventilation, and any carpentry for a dedicated space. Premium barrel saunas or custom builds can run far higher.

Running costs tell a subtler story. Infrared cabins draw less power per session — typically 1,500–1,750 watts — and sessions run 20–45 minutes at lower temperatures, so the electricity cost per session is modest. Traditional heaters draw more (often 4.5–9 kW), though sessions may be shorter. Either way, you can estimate your own numbers with our [session cost calculator](/tools/session-cost/), which factors in your local electricity rate and how often you plan to use the sauna.

The honest math: an infrared cabin costs less to buy, costs nothing to install, and costs slightly less to run. A traditional sauna costs more on all three fronts but delivers a more intense experience.

## Space and placement

Both types need a dry, ventilated indoor spot — a spare bedroom, finished basement, or garage corner. But infrared cabins are more forgiving. Many 1–2 person models are designed to tuck into a corner of a room with standard ceiling height, and because they don't produce steam or humidity, they don't demand special moisture management beyond what's normal for the room. That makes them genuinely apartment-friendly in a way traditional saunas rarely are.

Traditional saunas need a bit more thought: the steam and humidity mean you want proper ventilation, and the higher operating temperatures mean clearances and heat-resistant materials matter more. Outdoor installations are common for traditional saunas (barrel saunas are the classic example), and that route brings its own considerations around weatherproofing and electrical runs. If you're weighing indoor vs. outdoor placement generally, our [indoor vs. outdoor infrared sauna](/posts/indoor-vs-outdoor-infrared-sauna/) guide covers the trade-offs that apply to both types.

Size-wise, both scale from snug 1-person units to roomy 3-person cabins. If you're deciding between capacities, our [1-person vs. 2-person infrared sauna](/posts/1-person-vs-2-person-infrared-sauna/) breakdown applies mostly to infrared, but the general advice holds: buy the size you'll actually use, not the size you fantasize about.

## Session style: what it actually feels like

An infrared session is slow and steady. You sit in 120–140°F air, the panels warm you directly, and over 20–45 minutes you work up a deep, soaking sweat. Most people read, listen to music, or just zone out. It's comfortable enough that longer sessions feel natural, and the lower temperature is friendlier to people who find extreme heat unpleasant.

A traditional session is more of a ritual. You sit in 150–195°F air, the heat hits hard, you throw water on the rocks for steam when you want to push it, and you typically stay 10–20 minutes before cooling down — often with a cold shower or a step outside — then go back in for another round. The contrast between hot and cold is a big part of the experience.

Neither is "better." But they're different enough that you should buy the one whose style appeals to you, not the one a blog post declares the winner. If you want a long, relaxed, low-effort sweat while you read a book, infrared. If you want the intense heat-and-steam ritual with cooldowns between rounds, traditional.

## Maintenance

Infrared cabins are low-maintenance: wipe down the benches and walls periodically, keep the wood dry, and that's essentially it. No rocks to replace, no heater elements to service regularly, no humidity to manage. The electronics are the main long-term consideration — panels and control units can eventually need attention, which is one reason buying from an established brand with real warranty support matters.

Traditional saunas need a little more upkeep: the rocks degrade over time and need rearranging or replacement, the heater elements have a finite life, and the higher heat and humidity cycles put more wear on the wood. It's all routine stuff for sauna owners, but it is more stuff than an infrared cabin asks of you.

## Infrared-specific consideration: EMF

One thing infrared buyers should know and traditional buyers don't need to think about: electromagnetic fields. Infrared panels emit EMFs, and while the science on health effects is debated, many buyers reasonably prefer lower-EMF designs — especially since you'll be sitting inches from the panels. Look for models that advertise low-EMF or ultra-low-EMF construction and check whether the manufacturer publishes actual readings. Our [low-EMF infrared sauna guide](/posts/low-emf-infrared-sauna-guide/) explains what the numbers mean and how to evaluate the claims.

Traditional saunas don't raise this question at all — but they do run at far higher temperatures, which is its own consideration. Each type has its trade-offs; neither is free of them.

Buy infrared if you want: easy plug-and-play installation, a lower entry price (~$1,199–$2,500), gentler heat for longer sessions, and something that works in an apartment or spare bedroom.
