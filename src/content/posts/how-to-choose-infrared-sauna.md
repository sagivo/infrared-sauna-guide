Buying an infrared sauna comes down to seven decisions. Get these right and the specific model almost picks itself.

## 1. Size and capacity

Measure your space first — floor footprint, ceiling height, and door swing for assembly. Then be honest: will you sauna with a partner regularly, or is "2-person" aspirational? Our [1-person vs 2-person comparison](/posts/1-person-vs-2-person-infrared-sauna/) walks through the trade-offs, but the short version: buy for your real habits. A roomy solo cabin beats a cramped two-person one you'll use alone.

## 2. Heater type

Infrared saunas use far-infrared panels, and in this price tier those panels are almost always carbon or carbon-nano — large, flat panels that emit even heat at moderate surface temperatures. Older or cheaper designs use ceramic rod heaters, which run hotter in concentrated spots. We compared specs across this guide: every cabin here uses carbon-family panels, which is what you want.

## 3. EMF levels

Electromagnetic field exposure is the category's biggest anxiety, and manufacturers respond with "low EMF" and "ultra-low EMF" designations. The honest truth: most brands don't publish actual milligauss figures, so the labels aren't directly comparable. The exception in this guide is JNH, which quotes 2–3 mG at 6 inches for the Joyous. Our [low-EMF guide](/posts/low-emf-infrared-sauna-guide/) explains what the numbers mean and which models publish them.

## 4. Wood: hemlock vs. cedar

Canadian hemlock dominates the Amazon tier — pale, tight-grained, light, affordable, and nearly scentless. Canadian red cedar (only the SunRay Sedona here) is darker, aromatic, naturally moisture-resistant, and more expensive. Neither is "better" at conducting infrared heat; it's about scent, looks, durability, and price.

## 5. Electrical

Nine of the ten models in this guide run on a standard 120V/15A household outlet — true plug-and-play. The lone exception is the HeatWave Santa Fe, which needs a 120V/20A circuit. A dedicated circuit is recommended even for 15A models so a shared breaker doesn't trip mid-session. Full details in our [electrical requirements guide](/posts/infrared-sauna-electrical-requirements/).

## 6. Max temperature

Infrared cabins typically top out around 135–150°F — lower than traditional steam saunas, by design. The heat works differently: far-infrared warms your body directly rather than superheating the air. Don't chase the highest number; the 118–132°F working range that Dynamic quotes for the Venice Elite is where most owners actually sauna.

## 7. Warranty

Warranties in this guide run 2–7 years. Length is a reasonable proxy for how much faith the manufacturer has in its heaters and electronics. The Radiant Coronado and HeatWave Santa Fe lead at 7 years; JNH trails at 2. Weigh it against price — a longer warranty on a cabin you'll own for a decade is worth real money.

## Putting it together

Start with space and habits, filter by electrical (can you add a 20A circuit or not?), then compare heaters, EMF transparency, wood, and warranty within your shortlist.
