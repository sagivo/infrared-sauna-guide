Open any infrared sauna listing and the wood is always named: "Canadian hemlock" or "Canadian red cedar." Sellers treat it as a headline feature, and buyers treat it as a quality signal — cedar sounds premium, hemlock sounds like the budget option.

The reality is more even-handed than the marketing. Here's what each wood actually does inside a dry infrared cabin, when the premium is worth it, and the build details that matter more than either species.

## The short answer

**Hemlock** is pale, close-grained, scent-neutral, and hypoallergenic — and it costs less. **Cedar** is darker and richer-looking, carries the classic sauna aroma, and resists moisture and decay far better — and it costs more. For an **indoor infrared cabin** (dry heat, no rain, bare skin near the walls), hemlock's neutrality is a legitimate engineering choice. For an **outdoor sauna** taking weather, cedar's durability earns its premium. Since every cabin in this guide is indoor, most buyers should treat wood as a tiebreaker, not a decision-maker.

## Hemlock: the quiet standard

Canadian hemlock is the workhorse of the infrared sauna industry — and for indoor use, it's arguably the better-designed choice:

- **Scent-neutral and resin-free.** Hemlock contains almost no aromatic oils or resins. At 130–150°F, with your bare back against the wall, nothing outgasses onto your skin or into your airways. For anyone with allergies, asthma, or scent sensitivity, this is the safer pick.
- **Hypoallergenic.** Hemlock is widely described by sauna manufacturers as the low-allergen wood — no aromatic compounds to trigger itchy skin, streaming sinuses, or heat headaches, which a meaningful minority of users report with cedar.
- **Excellent insulator.** Hemlock's density and grain structure hold heat well while keeping the bench comfortable to sit on — relevant in an infrared cabin where you sit close to the panels.
- **Clean, light look.** Pale and close-grained with a uniform Scandinavian feel. It blends into a spare room or basement rather than announcing itself.
- **Lower cost.** Hemlock lumber is cheaper than western red cedar, and finished hemlock cabins typically run a few hundred dollars less than comparable cedar models.

The one honest knock on hemlock: it's **not durable outdoors**. It doesn't resist decay or insects the way cedar does, so a hemlock cabin belongs indoors. Since the cabins in this guide are all indoor units, that's a non-issue here — but don't put a hemlock cabin on an uncovered patio.

## Cedar: the aromatic premium

Western red cedar is the traditional sauna wood, and it brings real qualities — plus one expectation worth deflating:

- **The aroma.** Cedar's celebrated scent comes from its natural oils. But here's the deflation: in a *properly constructed* cedar sauna, the wood is kiln-dried at temperatures that burn off much of the resin and oil — precisely so those compounds don't outgas during sessions. The result smells far less like a cedar chest than buyers expect. The aroma is real but subtle.
- **Moisture and decay resistance.** This is cedar's genuine superpower: natural resistance to rot, moisture, and insects. For an outdoor sauna in wet weather, it's the right material and worth the premium. Indoors, in dry infrared heat, the advantage mostly goes unused.
- **Rich, dark appearance.** Cedar's reddish-brown grain is objectively beautiful — the classic sauna look. If the cabin sits in a living space where you'll see it daily, aesthetics count.
- **Softer wood.** Cedar dents and marks more easily than hemlock. In a cabin you'll assemble, move, and bump into, that shows over time.
- **The irritation caveat.** Those same aromatic oils that smell wonderful are irritants for some people — itchy skin, runny nose, headache at heat. Most users are fine, but if you're scent-sensitive, this is a real consideration, not a footnote.
- **Higher price.** The cedar premium is consistent: expect to pay more for the same size and heater count.

## The indoor-infrared verdict

Put the two profiles together and the honest conclusion for this guide's buyers: **for an indoor infrared cabin, wood species is close to a wash.** The dry, rain-free environment neutralizes cedar's biggest real advantage (weather resistance), and kiln-drying mutes its biggest emotional advantage (aroma). What remains is aesthetics, softness, a higher price, and a small irritation risk — against hemlock's neutrality, hardness, hypoallergenic profile, and lower cost.

That's not a knock on cedar. The [SunRay Sedona 1-Person](amazon:sunray-sedona-1-person) (~$1,799–$2,799) — the one cedar cabin in this guide — is a well-built unit with Canadian red cedar, a 7-year structural warranty, and the lowest wattage (1,290W) of any wooden cabin here, making it the cheapest to run. Buyers who want real cedar have a solid option. But the reason to choose it is that you want cedar, not that cedar heats better — it doesn't.

## What matters more than the wood

Whatever the species, these build details decide whether a cabin lasts:

1. **Panel joinery.** Look for tongue-and-groove or properly jointed solid panels — not veneered chipboard or thin cladding over a frame. The panel system is the cabin's structure.
2. **Unvarnished interior.** Sealed wood can't breathe, and coatings have no place against heat and skin. A quality cabin leaves the interior unfinished.
3. **Bench sturdiness.** The bench carries your full weight daily. A solid bench that doesn't flex matters more than whether it's hemlock or cedar.
4. **Heater count and placement.** Wood doesn't heat you — panels do. A well-distributed 7-heater hemlock cabin outperforms a 5-heater cedar cabin every time. See our [low-EMF guide](/posts/low-emf-infrared-sauna-guide/) for which models publish real output figures.
5. **Warranty on the wood.** The [SunRay Sedona's 7-year structural warranty](amazon:sunray-sedona-1-person) and the [Radiant Coronado's 7-year wood warranty](amazon:radiant-coronado-2-person) tell you more about build confidence than the species name does.

## Price reality in this guide

Across the cabins we track, hemlock dominates the Amazon $400–$2,500 tier — every model in our [best-infrared-saunas ranking](/posts/best-infrared-saunas-2026/) except the Sedona is Canadian hemlock, from the ~$1,199–$1,899 [JNH Joyous](amazon:jnh-joyous-2-person) to the ~$2,199–$2,799 [Dynamic Madrid](amazon:dynamic-madrid-3-person). Cedar appears at the top of the price ladder: the Sedona's ~$1,799–$2,799 range buys you real red cedar in a 1-person cabin — comparable hemlock 1-person cabins run hundreds less. Check current prices before buying; the cedar premium moves with lumber markets.

## The bottom line

Choose hemlock if you want the rational indoor pick: hypoallergenic, scent-free, hard-wearing, and cheaper — it's the default for a reason, and it's what nearly every cabin in this guide uses. Choose cedar if you want the traditional look and aroma and accept the premium — the [SunRay Sedona](amazon:sunray-sedona-1-person) is the way to get it in this tier. Either way, spend your comparison energy on panel joinery, heater layout, [electrical requirements](/posts/infrared-sauna-electrical-requirements/), and [EMF transparency](/posts/low-emf-infrared-sauna-guide/) — those decide your daily experience far more than the species badge on the wall. And if the cabin's going in a tight room, [size and placement](/posts/infrared-sauna-for-small-apartments/) matter more than wood too.
