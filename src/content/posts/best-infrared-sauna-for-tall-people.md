Most infrared sauna listings are written for average-height buyers. They lead with wattage, heater count, and price — and bury the one measurement that decides whether you fit comfortably: interior height.

If you're over 6'2", a cabin that looks spacious in photos can still put your head inches from the ceiling. Here's how to shop for headroom, which cabins in this guide actually deliver it, and which ones to skip.

## Why exterior height lies to you

Nearly every wooden infrared cabin in the Amazon $400–$2,500 tier stands about 75 inches tall — that's the industry-standard exterior dimension. But exterior height includes the base, the roof assembly, and wall thickness. Interior height is what your head lives under, and it typically runs **4 to 6 inches shorter** — roughly 69 to 71 inches inside a 75-inch cabin.

That's plenty for someone at 5'10". At 6'2" (74 inches), you are taller than the interior is tall — you fit only because you sit, not stand, and only if the bench is low enough to buy you a few extra inches. This is why "75-inch tall sauna" in the listing is not an answer; the interior measurement is.

The second trap: listings sometimes state an "applicable height range" instead of interior dimensions. A common one is **4.9 to 6.2 feet**. If the listing says the cabin suits users up to 6.2 feet, a 6'3" buyer should treat that as a warning flag, not a suggestion.

## The 5 measurements to check before you buy

1. **Interior height** — the headline number. Under 69 inches inside, a 6'2"+ user will feel cramped; 70+ is comfortable.
2. **Bench depth and width** — headroom is only half the fit. A deep bench lets long legs stretch; a narrow one forces a knees-up posture for the whole session.
3. **Door opening height** — tall users duck through the doorway every single session. A low door gets old fast and can be a real bump hazard when you're heat-drowsy.
4. **Heater placement relative to the bench** — overhead panels sit near the ceiling, which is exactly where a tall user's head goes. Back and calf panels scale fine with height; top panels don't.
5. **Your room's ceiling height** — the cabin needs assembly clearance above its exterior height, and low basement ceilings (under 7 feet) make tall cabins a tight squeeze. Plan for a few inches of working room above the roof.

## EMF matters more when you're tall

Here's the physics nobody mentions in the tall-person discussion: infrared heater panels on the ceiling or upper walls are fixed in place. A 6'4" user's head sits several inches closer to those panels than a 5'8" user's head does. Electromagnetic field strength increases as distance shrinks, so the same "low-EMF" cabin exposes a tall user to meaningfully more field than the average buyer the badge was designed around.

That doesn't mean tall users should avoid infrared saunas — it means the low-EMF claims deserve more scrutiny, not less. Our [low-EMF guide](/posts/low-emf-infrared-sauna-guide/) explains what the milligauss numbers mean and which models publish real measurements instead of badges. If you're tall and deciding between two otherwise equal cabins, take the one with the published EMF figure.

## The heat experience at height

One more consideration: heat rises, even radiant heat distributes unevenly. In a cabin sized for average users, a tall person's upper body sits in the warmest zone while their feet sit in the coolest — the gradient is steeper for you than for shorter users. Cabins with calf and foot heaters (most of the cabins in this guide include them) even out the gradient noticeably, so favor models with heaters at multiple heights rather than a single bank behind the back. The [JNH Joyous's seven heaters](amazon:jnh-joyous-2-person) and the [Santa Fe's door panel](amazon:heatwave-santa-fe-3-person) are good examples of multi-zone layouts.
